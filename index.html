02 / work

What I build

Backend and distributed systems, mostly Java and Kafka, mostly under someone else's SLA. The interesting part is always what happens when things fail.

01

ConfigX: dynamic config without redeploys

A Kubernetes sidecar platform that pushes configuration changes into running pods without a redeploy, coordinated through ZooKeeper.

  • The problem: changing a feature flag or threshold meant a full rollout, which is slow when you're mid-incident and risky when the change is one line.
  • The approach: a sidecar next to each service watches ZooKeeper and delivers updates to the app locally, so services never talk to the config store directly.
  • The hard part: keeping a fleet consistent. The platform has to handle a node that misses an update, a partial rollout, and a bad value that needs rolling back fast.
KubernetesSidecar patternZooKeeperWatches
02

JVM and GC tuning inside containers

Tuning garbage collection for services running under container CPU and memory limits, across G1GC and ZGC, generational heap sizing and container-aware ergonomics.

  • The problem: the JVM's defaults assume it owns the machine. Inside a cgroup, that leads to heaps sized wrong, GC threads fighting over a CPU quota, and pods OOM-killed with no Java-level error.
  • The approach: measure pause times and allocation rates under realistic load, then pick the collector and heap layout per service rather than using one fleet-wide setting.
G1GCZGCcgroupsContainer ergonomics
03

Compliance tokenization at ~2B records

A batch pipeline that tokenizes sensitive fields across roughly two billion records for regulatory compliance.

  • The problem: at this volume, a job that isn't restartable from the middle isn't a job. It's a gamble.
  • The approach: chunked, checkpointed processing where every chunk is safe to re-run, so a failure costs minutes rather than a day and never double-tokenizes a field.
Batch processingCheckpointingComplianceJava
04

Spring Boot 2 → 3, zero downtime

Migrated production services from Spring Boot 2 to 3 with no maintenance window and no dropped traffic.

  • The problem: Boot 3 means Jakarta EE namespaces, Java 17+, and changed behaviour in libraries you didn't know you depended on.
  • The approach: migrate service by service, run old and new side by side, and move traffic gradually so any regression shows up in a small slice first.
Spring Boot 3Jakarta EEZero-downtime
05

Kafka rebalancing and idempotency

Consumer rebalancing behaviour and idempotency strategies for Kafka-based services, so retries and partition reassignment don't turn into duplicate work.

  • The problem: "at-least-once" means "sometimes twice". When an event triggers a real-world action, processing it twice is a real mistake, not just a log line.
  • The approach: idempotency keys, careful offset-commit ordering, and rebalance listeners that finish in-flight work before a partition moves.
  • Why I care: this is exactly the problem payment systems and blockchains solve for money. It's how I got interested in finance infrastructure.
KafkaIdempotencyConsumer groupsExactly-once semantics

03 / markets

Why I'm optimistic about finance and crypto.

I'm not a trader and I don't sell anything. I'm an engineer who reads a lot about markets and has become structurally optimistic about where finance is going. Money is becoming software. Settlement is getting faster, custody is becoming a choice rather than a default, and a lot of financial plumbing built in the 1970s is being rebuilt as open, programmable infrastructure.

India is the clearest proof that this works. UPI took real-time payments from niche to default in a few years, and I've watched people in small towns who never had a card pay for chai with a QR code. Crypto is the same idea applied globally and without permission: a shared ledger anyone can build on. It's early, messy and full of bad actors, and I'm still optimistic, because the underlying engineering is sound and it keeps getting better.

"Short-term sceptic, long-term optimist. Prices are noise; settlement guarantees are signal."

— how I try to think about it

What I'm betting on

thesis 01

Bitcoin as a neutral reserve asset

A fixed supply, a predictable issuance schedule and no issuer to pressure. Whatever the price does in a given year, the idea of a savings asset that no single government controls isn't going away.

thesis 02

Stablecoins as payment rails

The most boring part of crypto is the one with real usage: dollars that move globally in seconds, around the clock. Cross-border payments and remittances are where this wins first.

thesis 03

Programmable settlement

Smart-contract platforms turn escrow, clearing and collateral into code that anyone can audit. That's a better starting point than trusting a black box, even if the tooling is still young.

thesis 04

Tokenised real-world assets

Bonds, funds and eventually equities on shared ledgers, with T+0 settlement and 24/7 markets. Traditional institutions are already building this, which tells you where it's heading.

thesis 05

India's financial stack

UPI, Aadhaar-based KYC and account aggregators are public digital infrastructure other countries are copying. I'm long on the Indian economy compounding on top of it.

thesis 06

Self-custody as a skill

Holding your own keys is operational security, not ideology. It's hard to do well, and the products that make it easy for normal people will be very valuable.

How I invest

  1. Time in the market beats timing it. Regular, automatic investing (SIPs for equities, DCA for crypto) removes most of my ability to make emotional mistakes.
  2. Size positions to sleep well. Crypto is a high-conviction, small-allocation bet. Everything else sits in boring, diversified index funds.
  3. Understand it or don't own it. If I can't explain how a protocol makes money or where its yield comes from, the yield is me.
  4. Not your keys, not your coins. Long-term holdings stay in self-custody. Exchanges are for moving money in and out, not for storing it.
  5. Account for the tax. In India, gains on crypto are taxed at a flat 30% with 1% TDS on transfers. Any thesis has to hold up after that.

05 / terrain

Away from the terminal

I'm an outdoor person first. Training outside is the best counterweight I've found to a job done sitting down.

current focus

Calisthenics

Bodyweight strength training is my main thing right now. All it needs is a bar, a patch of ground and consistency, so it goes wherever I go: a park in Bengaluru or a playground bar in a city I arrived in that morning, the session is the same. It's also the most honest kind of training: you can't cheat a strict pull-up, and progress is measured in skills you either have or don't.

  • Outdoors, not in a gym. Parks, bars and open ground, ideally early in the morning.
  • Progressions, not weights. You make an exercise harder by changing leverage and control, not by adding plates.
  • Travel-proof. No equipment means no excuse. A two-week trip doesn't reset progress.
  • Carries over. Grip, pulling strength and core are exactly what mountaineering and long hikes demand.

Mountaineering

Everything else I do is decent training for this. It's slow, self-reliant and unforgiving of bad planning. It's also the best lesson in risk management I know: the summit is optional, getting down isn't.

Football

Weekly and competitive, and the only thing on this list that's a team activity. The handle on my email gives away the club.

Running

Outdoors only, early in the morning, before the heat or the meetings start. It's also the fastest way to learn a new city: the first run in any place is the reconnaissance.
